# What is Rennet?

Rennet is, broadly speaking, two enzymes which assist in forming a milk curd from liquid milk. If milk is heated to approximately 185 F and some acid is added it will form a curd as well, but that is weaker and does not have the same texture and properties of a curd formed by using rennet.

An enzyme is a molecule that helps to break apart, or join together, or otherwise transform another molecule; enzymes are constantly used in our bodies and in all biological living entities to perform many critical functions of life. Rennet is composed of two enzymes, chymosin and pepsin, which help to bind together the protein/fat combination micelles in milk into a matrix-like structure that forms the bulk of a milk curd. Chymosin is the primary enzyme, and constitutes about 95% of natural rennet, while pepsin is only about 5% of natural rennet. Only chymosin is needed to form a curd, so many commercially-sold rennets are purified to contain either only chymosin, or closer to 100% than what is naturally occurring.

# How Does Rennet Work?

Simply put, rennet glues milk micelles together with calcium ions.

As in life and politics, the real story is more complex. [Read up on the structure of milk](/Milk/Composition) to understand the structure of milk micelles further. Chymosin works in two stages. Micelles have spike-like structures sticking out from their surface with a positive charge, so that as one micelle nears another the positive charges will repel each other and push the micelles apart. This allows the micelles to stay suspended pretty evenly throughout the milk, keeping the milk viscosity low and very liquidy. The first thing chymosin does is to bind to these positively-charged structures on the surface of the micelle, and chemically detach them to shear them off so that the micelles no longer repel each other. Next, chymosin will grab a free calcium ion that is dissolved in the milk and stick it to the micelle and then that same calcium ion will get stuck to another micelle. This process repeats until a great many micelles are stuck together, forming a curd. This is called *coagulation*.

Inside of these stuck-together-micelle structures there will be trapped water, minerals, other ions and components of the milk, and so on. As the curds are further cut, stirred and so forth the liquid and some of the other components will leak out of the curd and the curds will shrink and become more “dry”, an essential part of making aged cheeses.

Rennet works best within a specific temperature range, and within a certain pH (acidity) range. It works faster (up to a point) with higher temperatures, for example at 104 F it can coagulate milk at a rate almost double the speed which it does at 88 F. Milk which has not been acidified often has a natural pH of about 6.6-6.8, but if rennet is added when it has dropped down to 6.5-6.6, the rennet will work faster than when it is at 6.7-6.8. Some cheese making recipes call for letting the milk acidify via the cultures for a while first, in part to get the pH to drop a little so that the rennet works at a rate that is expected.

Typically, a good goal of time for curd coagulation is to see *flocculation* occur in 12-15 minutes, and then final coagulation of the milk curd after that depending on the particular recipe. Flocculation is the first sign that the rennet enzyme is well on its way to forming the curd, where little tiny beads of milk curd (*floccules*) will coat a knife but the curd is not yet fully formed.

When rennet is added to the milk, it should be diluted in cold unchlorinated water and poured evenly into the milk. It should be stirred in with mostly up-and-down motions for a minute or so to ensure it gets very evenly distributed throughout the milk, and then any motion and flow within the milk should be stopped (and the timer started if you are using the flocculation multiplier method, see below).

The milk should be “held quiet” and be undisturbed while the rennet does its work to help ensure a strong curd. Avoid bumping the vat, walking on the floor near it if it may send vibrations into the milk, etc.

## Curd Readiness

### Clean Break

There are two typical methods for checking if the rennet has finished coagulating the milk into a suitable curd. The first and easiest for beginners is to check for a “clean break”. If a knife is inserted into the curd with the blade vertical, but the knife angled at about 45 degrees with the curd surface, and then lifted up a little, the knife cut will separate. If it continues separating past the cut with a nice clean continuation, without the curd falling apart or the whey that fills into the cut being too milky, that is a clean break. 

### Flocculation Multiplier

The other method, which is more controllable and helps produce consistent results, is to test the curd for flocculation. When little doctors floccules begin showing up on a knife edge, or when a milk bottle cap or small and light bowl set on the surface of the milk stops behind able to spin freely, that time from when you added the rennet to that moment is the flocculation time. Then you wait more time for the curd to fully form based on a multiplier of that time. For example, if a Gouda cheese uses a flocculation multiplier of 3, then you time how long it takes form when you added rennet to flocculation. If that took 14 minutes, then the total time is 3x as long, in this example it would be 42 minutes. So when 42 minutes from when the rennet was added have passed, you would begin cutting your curds and moving on with the recipe. 

Using flocculation multipliers had the advantage that if you milk varies, or the temperature varies a little, or the rennet activity varies a little, and so on, those variations will result in a slightly different flocculation time, but overall you will end up with approximately the same curd strength each time you make the cheese despite the small variations in time. 

Some cheeses needs to have the curds cut when they are very fragile (for example, Parmesan styles), and some need it cut when the curds are much stronger and can retain much more moisture in their matrix (for example, blue cheeses). Knowing a flocculation multiplier allows you to tune the coagulation time to the particular cheese style.

## Rennet Strength

Different rennets have differing strength, depending on the purity of the chymosin enzyme and a few other factors.  In an effort to quantify and standardize the strength of the various sources of rennet, the cheese making industry came up with a standard called *international milk clotting units*, or IMCU. The definition of one IMCU is that it will coagulate 10 ml of pasteurized milk in 40 minutes at a temperature of 95 F / 35 C. Scaled up, this represents 100 IMCU to coagulate 1 L of milk under those same conditions. 

There are various rennet products sold as *single strength* and *double strength* rennet, although there unfortunately is no standard on that those terms mean. For a liquid rennet, it usually means either 200 IMCU per ml of liquid or 300 IMCU/ml, but it depends on the manufacturer and even the product line. For double strength, it varies pretty widely, but conceptually it will be around double the IMCUs of what a corresponding product's single strength version is.

Most products have a datasheet of some sort you can get from the manufacturer that will tell you the IMCU/ml (for liquid or paste) or the IMCU/g (for tablet or powder). You have to do a bit of math to calculate the equivalent for the rennet that is recommended in a given recipe, if the recipe specifies that at all. Otherwise, look for a recommended flocculation time in the recipe and then see how your particular rennet performs relative to that, and adjust it for the next time to get it closer to the ideal time stated in the recipe.

The flocculation multiplier method helps make this process easier. If the flocculation time is wrong, take the time it took and divide it by the time desired to find the amount of rennet to use the next time. E.g if you wanted at flocculation time of 15 minutes, but it took 25 minutes, you would use 25/15 or about 1.67x as much rennet the next time to get closer to the 15 minutes you were aiming for. From that, if you know the rennet the recipe called for is 300 IMCU/ml and needs 3 ml, once you have an equivalent amount of your rennet flocculating the milk in the same time, you know that amount of your rennet you used has approximately 3\*300 IMCU or 900 IMCU.

## Raw vs Pasteurized Milk and Homogenization

Pasteurized milk unfortunately has some of the calcium ions precipitate out of the milk when it got heated, so it typically needs some calcium added back in to help it form a good curd (this is usually done by adding [calcium chloride](/Cheese/Calcium-Chloride) to the milk). But even so, it takes more rennet to coagulate pasteurized milk than raw milk. The standards for rennet, and for most cheese making recipes are based around pasteurized milk. Unless the recipe states otherwise, you should assume the rennet amounts called for are for pasteurized milk.

Rennet also is affected by homogenization of milk. Curds will not form as well for milk that has been homogenized, and so calcium chloride should be added to help with curd formation. Generally, homogenized milks will form a more fragile curd, and the curds should be handled extra gently especially at first.

Typically, that means for raw milk you can use 25-50% less rennet and still get the same flocculation time. Usually this is 33% less. Different raw milks or even at different times it the year will respond more or less to rennet, so you may need to make some adjustments based on your particular milk.

## Cow vs Non-Cow Milk

Milk coming from ruminants other than cows may also need less rennet; sheep/ewe milk in particular needs about 20% less rennet due to its high amount of milk solids. If you combine the two recommendations where you use raw ewe's milk in a recipe based on pasteurized cow's milk, you end up needing approximately half the amount of rennet called for in the recipe.

Because cow milk naturally has the milk fat / cream rise, it is desirable to achieve flocculation within about 15 minutes so the milk fat can be locked into the protein matrix before the cream separates too much. Non-cow milks do not have this issue, so longer flocculation times may not be as much of a concern.

# Forms of Rennet

## Liquid

This form of rennet typically comes in plastic bottles as a dark liquid. It can come in single- and double-strength versions, with the single strength sometimes being 200 IMCU/ml or 300-315 IMCU/ml depending on the product. It should be stored in the fridge, never the freezer. It loses strength over time, where after a year or is expected that it will not coagulate milk as readily.

It should be diluted in cold, non-chlorinated water prior to stirring it into your milk. Don't leave it sitting out a long time before you stir it in.

## Paste

This is a concentrated form of liquid rennet, usually needing a lot less than liquid forms. It needs to be diluted in cold unchlorinated water prior to being added to the milk.

## Tablets

Tablet rennet is really just a pressed form of powder rennet. Each tablet has a recommended amount of milk it will coagulate. A tablet should be dissolved in cold unchlorinated water prior to being added to the milk. WalcoRen sells a tablet that is designed for approximately 4 L or 1 gallon of milk.

## Powder

Powder rennet is highly concentrated, usually needing only a small amount to get to the IMCUs desired. As in the other forms, it must be dissolved in cold unchlorinated water prior to being added to the milk.

# Types of Rennet

## Natural Rennet

The traditional source of rennet comes from the abomasum (4th stomach) of young ruminants: calves, goat kids, and lambs. The stomachs are harvested, dried in salt, and ground into powder. This powder contains rennet as well as some other enzymes, notably lipase which breaks down fat into aromatic compounds. Many commercial natural rennets sold today are purified so that they contain almost exclusively just chymosin, so that a cheese maker can choose more deliberately how much lipase they add (if any) and so that the coagulation process is more predictable.

For traditional and artisanal cheese making, natural rennet is usually the rennet of choice. Many makers claim that it produces the best tasting cheese. It can be slightly market-limiting however due to the fact that it is neither vegetarian, kosher, nor halal.

## Microbial Rennet

Some microbes are able to produce chymosin, particularly Rhizomucor miehei, and so a fermentation process is used to produce the rennet. It must be purified, and sometimes has some mold byproducts that can affect the flavor of the final cheese come through.

## Fermentation Produced Rennet (GMO)

Using modern genetic modification techniques, yeasts and E. coli variants are modified with rDNA to produce chymosin as a byproduct of their fermentation. The original genes for the production of chymosin are derived from calf DNA. The results of the fermentation are purified so that hopefully they contain very pure chymosin, even more so than natural rennets do even when they are also purified. It is claimed to be the most pure, and produce the least bitterness and the most predictability of all the types of rennet.

Controversially, the industry has claimed that the resulting rennet is itself “non-GMO” because the original bacteria or yeasts are killed and they claim to only retain the purified rennet enzyme. It is also claimed that it is vegetarian because no direct calf material is used. Finally, it is accepted in the jewish community as kosher, and in the muslim community as halal. Almost all of the mass-produced cheese on the market today uses FPC rennet as a result, but there are concerns about the purity of the rennet or the acceptance of the rennet itself as non-GMO, when it is clearly produced by GMO microorganisms.

## Plant-Derived Rennet

A few plants produce rennet or rennet-like enzymes that can assist in coagulating cheese. Of particular note is thistle rennet, which is used in some traditional cheese such as Cardoona cheese.
