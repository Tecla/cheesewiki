# Livestock Diseases and Treatments

## Mastitis

Mastitis is inflammation of the mammary gland, usually caused by bacterial infection entering through the teat canal. It is one of the most common problems in dairy animals, affecting cows, goats, and sheep. For cheesemakers, the concern is not only the animal's welfare: mastitic milk has a higher somatic cell count that reduces cheese yield, alters curd formation, and affects flavor.

### Identifying Mastitis, and the California Mastitis Test

Mastitis can be obvious (**clinical**) or hidden (**subclinical**). Clinical cases show visible signs:

- Swollen, hot, or painful udder
- Abnormal milk: watery, discolored, or containing clots, flakes, or strings
- Fever, lethargy, or loss of appetite
- The animal may kick at the milker or resist milking

Subclinical mastitis is far more common and produces milk that looks normal, but the animal is infected and shedding bacteria. This is where regular testing becomes important.

The **California Mastitis Test (CMT)** is a simple, inexpensive cow-side screen. It works by mixing a small amount of milk from each quarter with a reagent that causes somatic cells to clump and thicken. The thicker the mixture, the higher the somatic cell count and the more likely infection is present. A good visual guide and procedure can be found from [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/dairy-health-and-management/california-mastitis-test).

A few practical notes:

- Test each quarter separately. Infection is often quarter-specific.
- High CMT readings should be followed up with a milk culture to identify the bacteria involved.
- CMT is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. A veterinary diagnosis and culture are needed before starting most treatments.
- **Milk pH is a clue, not a mastitis test.** High pH milk (around 6.8–7.05 or higher) can cause slow pH drops during cheese making and poor coagulation, but it does not prove mastitis. Causes of elevated pH and high somatic cell counts include mastitis, stress, udder injury, stale milk from incomplete milking, and late lactation. For cheesemakers, a lab somatic cell count (SCC) is generally more useful than pH alone or even a negative culture. See the discussion at CheeseForum.org: [High pH and poor coagulation but negative on mastitis test!](https://cheeseforum.org/index.php?topic=19247.0).

Common mastitis-causing bacteria include:

- **Contagious pathogens** such as *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus agalactiae*, which spread primarily during milking via contaminated hands, equipment, or teat liners.
- **Environmental pathogens** such as *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, and *Streptococcus uberis*, which come from bedding, manure, mud, or contaminated water.
- **Mycoplasma**, which can cause outbreaks and is particularly frustrating because it does not respond to conventional antibiotics.

### Treatments and Remedies

Treatment depends on whether the mastitis is mild, severe, contagious, or environmental. The safest general rule is: **culture before you treat**, whenever possible. Treating blindly can waste money, create antibiotic resistance, and delay the right therapy.

**Conventional treatments:**

- **Intramammary antibiotics** are the standard for mild to moderate cases. They are infused directly into the affected quarter, usually after milking, for a set number of treatments.
- **Systemic antibiotics and anti-inflammatories** may be needed for severe cases with fever or whole-udder involvement.
- **Dry cow therapy** is a long-acting antibiotic infusion given at the end of lactation to clear up subclinical infections and prevent new ones during the dry period.
- **Milk withholding:** Milk from treated animals must be discarded for the full withdrawal period listed on the drug label. Residue testing is important if milk is being sold or used for cheese.

**Day-to-day management of a case:**

- Milk the affected quarter completely and frequently to reduce bacterial load and pressure.
- Keep the animal clean, dry, and comfortable. Wet, dirty bedding is a major risk factor.
- Keep infected animals last in the milking order, or use dedicated equipment, to avoid spreading contagious organisms.
- Cull chronically infected animals that do not respond to treatment, especially those with *Staphylococcus aureus* or Mycoplasma, because they can act as ongoing reservoirs of infection.

**Natural and supportive options:**

Some producers successfully treat mild mastitis without antibiotics using **probiotics**, **essential oils**, **homeopathic preparations**, or simple supportive care such as frequent stripping, massage, and improved hygiene. Formal studies of these as primary treatments are limited, but many farmers report good results in mild to moderate cases, and avoiding antibiotics helps reduce antibiotic resistance and preserves the animal's natural immune function. For cheesemakers especially, avoiding antibiotics means avoiding milk withholding and residue concerns. In severe or rapidly worsening cases — fever, whole-udder involvement, toxemia, or no response to natural methods within a reasonable window — conventional veterinary treatment should be used promptly.

Mastitis milk should not be used for cheese or milk intended for consumption. Even after recovery, milk quality should be checked before the animal returns to the milking string.

## Prion Diseases

Prion diseases are infectious agents that propagate through livestock systems via protein misfolding processes. Major manifestations include:

- **BSE (Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy)** – affects cattle and dairy cows
- **Scrapie** – affects sheep and goats
- **CWD (Chronic Wasting Disease)** – affects cervids (deer, elk, moose)

For details on BSE (also known as mad cow disease), see [Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) | Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/bse-mad-cow-disease-and-vcjd).

Mad cow disease, to date, has only been discovered in the UK, and there has never been a truly confirmed case of transfer to human beings. But it is a serious disease that should not be taken lightly, even if it is rare in domesticated herds and flocks.

> **Important:** When a prion disease is encountered in an animal in the herd or flock, the best course of action is to cull the animal and dispose of it off-property. There is no known cure for prion diseases, and the potential for spread to other animals and humans means the animal must be completely removed.

## Infectious Diseases

### Influenzas

Influenza viruses are a family of respiratory pathogens that primarily infect birds and mammals. Several strains are relevant to livestock keepers, especially those running mixed-species farms or keeping poultry near dairy animals.

**Avian influenza** (bird flu, including highly pathogenic strains such as H5N1 and H7N9) mainly affects poultry, but some strains have been found in or around cattle, goats, pigs, and other mammals. In dairy animals, influenza is usually a concern because a farm-wide outbreak disrupts milk production and may spread between species, not because it is a routine cattle disease.

**Swine influenza** affects pigs and is common in commercial and backyard hog operations. It causes coughing, fever, reduced appetite, and poor growth. Pigs can act as "mixing vessels" for influenza viruses, so farms with both pigs and poultry should be especially careful.

**Influenza D virus** has been identified in cattle and swine and is associated with mild respiratory disease. It does not appear to infect humans, but it can contribute to bovine respiratory disease complexes, especially when animals are stressed.

#### Signs to Watch For

Influenza in livestock typically looks like a respiratory illness:

- Fever
- Coughing, nasal discharge, or labored breathing
- Lethargy and reduced feed intake
- Drop in milk production in lactating animals
- Secondary bacterial pneumonia

Milk from sick animals should be handled carefully. Any animal with fever, respiratory signs, or abnormal milk should be considered suspect and milked last, with equipment cleaned thoroughly afterward. In a confirmed or suspected outbreak, milk from affected animals should not be used for raw-milk cheese or human consumption until a veterinarian clears it.

#### Managing It Naturally

Because influenza is viral, antibiotics do not treat the underlying infection. They may be justified only if a veterinarian diagnoses a secondary bacterial pneumonia. This makes influenza a good example of where natural, low-intervention management should come first:

- **Biosecurity is the first line of defense.** Limit visitors, keep wildlife and stray birds away from feed and water, and avoid sharing equipment between farms.
- **Separate species where practical, but use managed trailing when it helps.** You do not want chickens, turkeys, or pigs free-ranging through bedding or feed areas where ruminants congregate, because that is how pathogens and parasites spread. However, in a management-intensive grazing system, trailing poultry about two paddocks behind cattle is a well-known technique that *reduces* pest and pathogen pressure. The birds scatter manure pats, consume fly larvae, and break parasite cycles. The key is deliberate movement and timing, not constant cohabitation.
- **Reduce stress.** Crowding, long transport, poor ventilation, sudden weather changes, and nutritional deficits all increase susceptibility. Well-managed pasture and low-stress handling support the immune system.
- **Good nutrition and fresh air.** Animals on high-quality pasture with adequate minerals, especially selenium and vitamin E, generally handle respiratory challenges better than animals in damp, crowded confinement.
- **Quarantine new arrivals.** A simple isolation period for any new animal or one returning from shows, sales, or breeding lets you catch illness before it enters the herd.

#### When to Get the Vet

Call a veterinarian promptly when:

- Multiple animals become sick at the same time, especially with fever and respiratory signs.
- An animal has trouble breathing, is off feed, or has a sustained high fever.
- Milk production drops sharply across the herd.
- There is any suspicion of a reportable disease. Many jurisdictions require reporting of certain avian influenzas and other infectious diseases.

Veterinary guidance is also needed before using antiviral drugs, which are rarely appropriate in livestock, or before making culling decisions.

## Parasites

Parasites are an unavoidable part of keeping livestock, but their impact can be kept low through management. The goal is not to eliminate every parasite — that is neither possible nor desirable, since some exposure helps animals develop immunity — but to keep parasite loads below the level where they cause disease, poor condition, or reduced milk production.

### Internal Parasites

The internal parasites that cause the most trouble in dairy ruminants are **gastrointestinal worms**, especially the barber pole worm (*Haemonchus contortus*) in sheep and goats, and **coccidia** in young stock. Liver flukes can also be a problem in wet regions.

**Signs of a heavy worm load:**

- Pale gums or inner eyelids (anemia from blood-sucking worms)
- Bottle jaw or generalized edema
- Weight loss or poor growth despite good feed
- Diarrhea, especially in young animals
- Dull coat and reduced milk production
- Sudden death in severe cases

**Management and natural control:**

- **Pasture rotation and rest.** Moving animals before pasture plants are grazed too short, and giving paddocks long rest periods, breaks the parasite life cycle. Most worm larvae cannot survive long without a host, so 3–6 weeks of rest (longer in cool, wet weather) can dramatically reduce larval loads.
- **Avoid grazing young stock on contaminated pasture.** Calves, lambs, and kids are the most vulnerable. Graze them on pasture that has not held the same species recently, ideally after a hay cut or a long rest.
- **Keep forage height up.** Larvae concentrate in the lower few inches of pasture plants. Grazing higher residuals reduces intake of infective larvae.
- **Mixed-species grazing.** Cattle, sheep, and goats do not generally share the same worm species, so rotating ruminant species through paddocks can clean up pasture for one another. (Do be careful with coccidia and some flukes, which may cross species lines.)
- **Healthy immune systems.** Well-nourished animals with adequate copper, selenium, cobalt, and protein are far more resistant to parasites than undernourished animals. Good body condition and low stress are part of the defense.
- **Breed for resistance.** Over time, culling animals that repeatedly require deworming and keeping those that thrive with minimal intervention gradually improves the herd's or flock's natural parasite resistance. Treat the chronic offenders and sell or harvest them; keep the vigorous ones as breeders. In sheep, breeds such as **Katahdin** and **St. Croix** are known for strong parasite resistance, though their milk production is generally lower than traditional dairy breeds. That trade-off is part of what each farmer must decide. Combined with good management-intensive grazing, a breeding program for parasite resilience is one of the most durable long-term controls.

**Alternative dewormers:**

- **Diatomaceous earth (DE)** is popular as a feed supplement. Results are mixed: some farmers report benefits, but controlled studies have not consistently shown strong efficacy against established worm burdens. It may have a minor role as part of a broader program.
- **Botanical dewormers** such as black walnut, wormwood, garlic, and certain tannin-rich plants (sericea lespedeza, chicory, sainfoin) have been studied more in small ruminants. Tannin-rich forages show real promise for reducing fecal egg counts. These are best viewed as supportive tools, not replacements for monitoring and selective conventional treatment.
- **Nematode-trapping fungi** and other biological controls are promising but still mostly experimental for small farms.

**When conventional dewormers make sense:**

Use them strategically, not routinely. Blanket deworming creates resistant worms and wastes money. Good practice includes:

- Doing **fecal egg counts** (individual or pooled) to identify which animals actually need treatment.
- Treating only the animals with high egg counts or obvious clinical signs.
- Using the correct drug for the parasite present, at the correct dose, and confirming effectiveness with a follow-up fecal egg count reduction test.
- Keeping treated animals out of the milking string for the labeled withdrawal period, and never using milk from treated animals for raw-milk products until the withdrawal period has passed.

### External Parasites

Lice, mites, ticks, and flies cause irritation, blood loss, and can spread disease. They are usually easier to manage with hygiene and environmental control than with repeated chemical treatments.

**Lice and mites:**

- More common in winter and in crowded, dusty conditions.
- Cause rubbing, hair loss, scurf, and poor thrift.
- Treatment options range from sulfur dusts and diatomaceous earth to pour-on or injectable products. Severe infestations may need a conventional treatment, with appropriate milk and meat withdrawal.

**Ticks:**

- Vectors for diseases such as anaplasmosis and babesiosis in cattle.
- Pasture rest, brush control, and guinea fowl or chickens trailing behind cattle can reduce tick numbers.
- In high-tick regions, some producers use permethrin-based tags or rubs; others rely on breed resistance and pasture management.

**Flies:**

- Horn flies, face flies, and stable flies are more than a nuisance. Horn flies can cause significant blood loss and reduced milk production.
- Good manure management, dry bedding, and breaking up manure pats (through chickens trailing behind cattle, dung beetles, or harrowing) reduce fly breeding sites.
- Fly traps, wasp parasitoids, and essential-oil repellents can help. In severe cases, targeted use of insecticides may be warranted.

### Integrated Parasite Management

A durable parasite program combines several tools:

1. Monitor with fecal egg counts and visual signs.
2. Manage pasture to break parasite life cycles.
3. Maintain nutrition and mineral status so animals resist infection.
4. Select and breed for parasite resistance.
5. Use natural and biological controls where they are effective.
6. Reserve conventional dewormers and insecticides for targeted use, and rotate drug classes to slow resistance.
7. Record treatments and follow all withdrawal periods.

Parasite resistance is now a serious problem worldwide, largely because of decades of routine chemical deworming. A natural-first, selective approach protects not only the animals and the milk supply, but also the long-term effectiveness of the drugs that may be needed in a genuine crisis.

## Other Diseases

Other common problems in the dairy herd are metabolic, nutritional, or chronic rather than infectious. These are worth recognizing because they affect milk production, animal welfare, and whether milk is safe to use.

### Bloat

Bloat is a life-threatening accumulation of gas in the rumen. It is usually caused by eating too much lush, high-legume pasture (especially alfalfa or clover), finely ground grain, or feed that has fermented in the rumen too quickly.

**Signs:**

- Distended left flank, tight like a drum
- Difficulty breathing, frequent urination or defecation attempts
- Restlessness, staggering, or lying down and unable to rise
- Without relief, the animal can die within hours

**Prevention and management:**

- Introduce lush pasture or new feeds gradually, especially legumes.
- Avoid grazing legume-heavy pastures when they are wet with dew or rain.
- Provide dry hay before turning animals onto rich pasture.
- Keep anti-bloat oils (such as poloxalene or vegetable oils) available for high-risk situations.
- A frothy bloat may respond to vegetable or mineral oil drenches, or to passing a stomach tube to break the foam. A severe bloat with a tight rumen may require a trocar by a veterinarian or experienced stockman. Bloat is an emergency.

### Milk Fever (Hypocalcemia)

Milk fever is a calcium deficiency that most often strikes high-producing dairy cows around the time of calving, when calcium suddenly shifts into milk production. It is less common in goats and sheep.

**Signs:**

- Early: restlessness, stiffness, reduced feed intake
- Classic: the cow lies down with an S-shaped curve to her neck, cold ears, and a weak pulse
- Severe: coma and death

**Prevention and management:**

- Do not over-feed calcium before calving. The goal is to keep the cow's own calcium-regulating system active.
- A low-calcium diet in late pregnancy, followed by high calcium after calving, helps many animals regulate properly.
- Oral calcium drenches, gels, or boluses at calving and 12 hours later are common preventive tools.
- Intravenous calcium from a veterinarian is the standard treatment for clinical milk fever. It must be given carefully and slowly because rapid calcium changes can stop the heart.

### Ketosis

Ketosis occurs when a cow or doe in early lactation cannot eat enough energy to match her milk production, so her body breaks down fat too quickly. The byproducts (ketones) build up in the blood.

**Signs:**

- Reduced appetite, especially for grain
- Weight loss despite milk production
- Sweet or fruity-smelling breath
- Lethargy, staggering, or unusual behavior
- In severe cases, nervous ketosis with head-pressing, circling, or aggression

**Prevention and management:**

- Manage body condition before calving; overly fat animals are more prone.
- Ensure fresh, palatable, high-energy feed after calving.
- Some producers provide propylene glycol or molasses as an oral energy source for at-risk animals.
- In severe cases, veterinary treatment with intravenous glucose and fluids is needed.

### Grass Tetany (Hypomagnesemia)

Grass tetany is a magnesium deficiency most often seen in cattle turned out onto rapidly growing spring pasture, especially pasture high in nitrogen or potassium. Lush grass can be low in available magnesium.

**Signs:**

- Excitability, muscle twitching
- Staggering, convulsions
- Death often follows quickly once signs appear

**Prevention and management:**

- Provide magnesium supplements before turnout and during high-risk periods. This can be done with free-choice minerals, magnesium boluses, or hay that contains adequate magnesium.
- Avoid grazing very young, rapidly growing grass exclusively.
- Treatment requires intravenous magnesium and calcium from a veterinarian; it is an emergency.

### Acidosis and Grain Overload

When ruminants eat too much starch and not enough fiber, the rumen becomes too acidic. This damages the rumen lining, kills beneficial microbes, and can cause laminitis, liver abscesses, and chronic poor thrift.

**Signs:**

- Off feed, diarrhea, especially with a foamy or watery consistency
- Sore feet or lameness
- Reduced milk fat and poor body condition

**Prevention and management:**

- Feed plenty of long-stem fiber and limit grain, especially finely ground grain.
- Make any feed changes gradually.
- Provide free-choice forage before and during high-starch feeding.
- Once acidosis occurs, remove grain, provide good-quality hay, and consult a veterinarian. Severe cases may need rumen transfaunation (introducing healthy rumen contents from another animal).

### Johne's Disease

Johne's (pronounced "Yo-nees") is a chronic, contagious bacterial infection of the intestinal tract caused by *Mycobacterium avium* subspecies *paratuberculosis*. It causes progressive weight loss and diarrhea despite a normal appetite, usually in animals two years or older.

Johne's is difficult to control on a dairy farm because there is no reliable cure, and it spreads through manure-contaminated feed, water, and colostrum. It is a major reason to buy animals from tested herds and to keep young stock away from adult manure.

**Management:**

- Test and cull positive animals.
- Do not feed pooled colostrum from untested dams to calves or kids.
- Improve hygiene, calving areas, and young-stock housing.
- Johne's is a long-term herd problem; prevention is far easier than control.

### Bovine Leukemia Virus (BLV)

BLV is a viral infection of cattle that causes leukemia and lymphoma over time. It is spread mainly through blood, including contaminated needles, dehorning equipment, and rectal palpation sleeves. It is common in many dairy herds.

The reason to control BLV is that it reduces milk production and longevity even before obvious tumors appear. Most authorities do not consider it a human health risk, but it is preventable through simple blood hygiene.

**Management:**

- Use single-use needles and clean equipment.
- Test and remove persistently infected animals if practical.
- Do not share instruments between animals without disinfection.

### Foot Rot

Foot rot is a bacterial infection of the hoof, usually caused by *Fusobacterium necrophorum* and *Dichelobacter nodosus*. It enters through softened or damaged hoof tissue, especially in wet, muddy conditions.

- Signs: sudden lameness, swelling between the toes, foul-smelling discharge, heat in the foot.
- Prevention: keep walkways and loafing areas dry and clean, trim hooves regularly, avoid overcrowding, and provide hard, abrasive surfaces that help wear hooves naturally.
- Treatment: clean and trim the hoof to expose the infected area, soak or wrap with a bactericidal solution, and move the animal to a dry area. Severe or persistent cases may need antibiotics under veterinary guidance.

### Pinkeye (Infectious Bovine Keratoconjunctivitis)

Pinkeye is a highly contagious bacterial infection of the eye, most often caused by *Moraxella bovis*. Face flies spread it from animal to animal.

- Signs: tearing, squinting, reddened conjunctiva, cloudiness or ulceration of the cornea; untreated cases can cause blindness.
- Prevention: fly control, shade to reduce eye irritation from bright sun and dust, clipping tall pasture seed heads that can scratch the eye, and prompt isolation of affected animals.
- Treatment: protect the eye from sunlight with a patch, use antibiotic eye ointments or sprays as appropriate, and in severe cases consult a veterinarian.

### Hardware Disease

Hardware disease occurs when a ruminant swallows a sharp metal object — often a piece of wire, nail, or baling staple — which can penetrate the reticulum and damage the heart or other organs.

- Signs: poor appetite, weight loss, signs of pain when lying down or rising, fever, sudden death if the object perforates the heart.
- Prevention: keep feed areas and pasture free of scrap metal, use magnetic cow magnets in high-risk settings, and inspect feed and forage equipment for wear that could shed metal.
- Treatment: a rumen magnet can sometimes retrieve loose metal; confirmed or suspected cases with a penetrating object require veterinary care, often surgery.

### Retained Placenta and Metritis

A retained placenta occurs when the afterbirth is not expelled within the normal window after calving, kidding, or lambing. It can lead to metritis, a uterine infection that threatens fertility and sometimes the animal's life.

- Signs of retained placenta: fetal membranes hanging from the vulva more than 12–24 hours after birth.
- Signs of metritis: foul-smelling discharge, fever, reduced milk, loss of appetite, depression.
- Prevention: good nutrition and body condition before calving, clean calving areas, adequate selenium and vitamin E, and avoiding obesity or extreme thinness.
- Treatment: do not pull on a retained placenta. Consult a veterinarian. Mild cases may resolve with hygiene and time; metritis usually requires uterine lavage and systemic antibiotics.

### Enterotoxemia / Overeating Disease

Enterotoxemia is a sudden, often fatal disease caused by toxins produced by *Clostridium perfringens* type C and D. It is most common in young, rapidly growing sheep and goats on lush feed, grain, or milk replacer.

- Signs: sudden death, abdominal pain, diarrhea, kicking at the belly, seizures.
- Prevention: gradual introduction of grain and lush pasture, good-quality forage, vaccination of dams and offspring, and avoiding overfeeding milk or grain to young stock.
- Treatment: often unsuccessful once signs appear. A veterinarian may administer antitoxin, antibiotics, anti-inflammatories, and supportive fluids. Prevention is far more effective than treatment.

## Prevention

Most livestock diseases respond better to prevention than to treatment. The patterns that show up across mastitis, influenza, parasites, and metabolic disease are the same: healthy animals on well-managed land are far less likely to get sick, and far more likely to recover quickly when they do.

In practice, that means:

- **Clean, dry, low-stress housing.** Wet bedding and crowded conditions breed bacteria, parasites, and respiratory disease.
- **High-quality pasture and feed.** Good nutrition, adequate minerals, and appropriate body condition support immunity, prevent metabolic disease, and reduce parasite susceptibility.
- **Clean milking and clean collection.** Clean teats, clean equipment, and prompt chilling protect both the animal and the milk supply.
- **Management-intensive grazing.** Moving animals frequently breaks parasite cycles, distributes manure, and lets you observe animals closely every day.
- **Quarantine and biosecurity.** New animals, returning show stock, and visitors can all bring disease onto the farm. A simple isolation period and clean boots or clothes reduce risk.
- **Observation and record keeping.** Daily observation catches illness early. Records of breeding, calving, treatments, and withdrawal periods make management decisions easier.
- **Natural-first, selective treatment.** Use the gentlest effective tool first — nutrition, hygiene, pasture management, and breeding for resilience. Reserve antibiotics, anthelmintics, and other interventions for cases that genuinely need them.
- **Know when to call for help.** Some conditions are emergencies or require a diagnosis that cannot be made at the fence line. Knowing a good veterinarian and calling early protects both the animals and the farm.

Prevention is not a single product or protocol. It is the sum of daily habits that keep the farm environment, the herd, and the milk healthy.
